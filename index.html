03 — Position

On the question of forty-nine

Forty-nine keys is not a design decision. It is forty-seven deferred ones, handed to you at one in the morning, in the dark, in front of people.

Every control on a surface is a question the designer declined to answer. Which one is the kick. Which one is the strobe. Which fader was the good one last time. A large controller does not give you options; it gives you a subscription to second-guessing, billed live, in front of an audience that came to dance.

The Nano answers both of its questions at the factory. There is a left one and a right one. In eighteen months of field use we have not once observed a performer look down.

We are aware of the counter-argument: that a controller should have a fader for everything. We have read it carefully. It has a lead time of five hundred days.

We considered three buttons. The prototype was returned to the drawer. Three introduces a middle, a middle introduces a preference, and a preference is just a fader that has not admitted it yet.

Two is not a limitation we accepted. It is the only number we could defend.

04 — Firmware

Written by the competition

The hard part is not ours. The pin map, the bootloader trick and the MIDI descriptor all come from Christos, who also designed the Classic. He turned a €2.79 macropad into a real class-compliant MIDI device over a weekend, and then published it under a licence that lets anyone do this. Commercially, we consider that his one mistake. We have put it in an app, so that repeating it no longer requires a compiler.

# the whole install unzip BitchBoyNano.zip open "BitchBoy Nano.app" # pick two notes, hit Flash

No host software. No keystroke-to-MIDI translator. No configurator that only runs on Windows. The pad enumerates as 1209:0001 “BitchBoy Nano” and every operating system made in the last twenty years already knows what to do with it.

One window configures the whole device — note or CC per key, channel per key, and what the lights do. If you want a sustain pedal instead of two notes, that is one dropdown and a reflash. The first flash needs a 10k resistor across two pins for a second; after that, hold both keys while plugging in.

It can also be a keyboard, obviously. Any key, any combo, a line of text, a whole recorded macro — save on the left, undo on the right. The Classic cannot do this. It is MIDI and only MIDI, which is principled, and also boring.

Flashing is one-way: the WCH bootloader writes and verifies flash but cannot read it, so the stock firmware is gone for good. The chip itself is unbrickable — its bootloader lives in mask ROM. Worst case, you flash it again.
